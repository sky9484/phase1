# Keep Sui for proof, not payouts

Sui works well as the place Splash keeps its records, approvals and evidence, and it fails as the network money leaves on. Every Southeast Asian and African payout in this research has to leave Sui: no licensed payout partner was verified to accept USDC on Sui, no ramp was verified to buy or sell USDC on Sui through FPX, GCash, QRIS or a Nigerian bank transfer, and Onramper, although it lists FPX on its Malaysia page, is documented by Sui itself as selling only the SUI token on Sui ([Sui docs](https://docs.sui.io/onchain-finance/asset-custody/fiat-on-ramps)). The main way native USDC leaves Sui is Circle's CCTP V1, and Sui and Noble are the only chains Circle still lists as V1-only ([Circle](https://developers.circle.com/cctp/cctp-supported-blockchains)); Circle cuts V1 burn limits from **31 October 2026** and pauses the contracts on **1 December** ([Circle blog](https://www.circle.com/blog/migrate-to-cctp-v2-ahead-of-cctp-v1-legacy-deprecation)), and its promise to bring V2 to Sui has already slipped once. Sui also holds a fraction of its rivals' money, **US$478.5M of stablecoins against US$3.84B on Arbitrum and US$16.69B on Solana** ([DefiLlama](https://stablecoins.llama.fi/stablecoinchains)), and lost more than 15 hours to three halts in May. Sui is stronger than Solana and Arbitrum on login identity that validators check on-chain (zkLogin), on free plain stablecoin transfers and on native Walrus and Seal, and its Move rules let an auditor read Splash's zero-balance rule from the bytecode, which makes it the right home for Splash's proof layer. The hub question comes out the same on every chain: the Philippines should be the main destination where licensed partners pay out, Indonesia second, and Malaysia the origin where Splash serves payers, while the Philippines–Africa idea is a trap, because Philippine exports to Nigeria were **US$7.02m** in 2025. The better plan keeps Sui for accounts, approvals, in-network transfers and evidence, sends payout money to licensed partners on the chains they accept, keeps Walrus as the single evidence store anchored on all three chains, charges for proof and payouts rather than transfers, and makes a tested exit from Sui the launch gate on 31 October.

## Sui's native strengths are real, and every one comes with a catch

**Sui's best payments feature is live, and neither Solana nor Arbitrum has a protocol-level equivalent.** Since 20 May 2026, wallet-to-wallet transfers of seven allowlisted stablecoins, native USDC and USDsui among them, cost **US$0.00** ([Sui blog](https://www.sui.io/blog/sui-launches-gasless-stablecoin-transfers)). The catch sits in the eligibility rules. A transaction qualifies only if it calls nothing but Sui's own balance and coin functions, writes no objects, sets gas price and budget to zero and moves at least 0.01 ([Sui docs](https://docs.sui.io/develop/transaction-payment/gasless-stablecoin-transfers)). Any call to `splash_core`, any evidence object, any custom event and any Payment Kit receipt turns it into a paid transaction. Sui's code does allow a Splash fee leg inside the same free transaction, but every recipient must net at least US$0.01 ([Sui code](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/MystenLabs/sui/main/sui-execution/latest/sui-adapter/src/temporary_store.rs)), so a 0.10% fee stays gasless only on payments of US$10 or more. The free capacity is shared by the whole network and capped at **300 transactions per second**, and a busy network serves gas-paying transactions first ([Sui protocol config](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/MystenLabs/sui/main/crates/sui-protocol-config/src/lib.rs)). "Stablecoin transfers on Sui can be gas-free" is true. "Splash payments are gasless" is false, and every payment needs a paid fallback.

**The same upgrade changed how money sits on Sui, and that creates two jobs.** Gasless transfers land in an address balance, a running total kept by the protocol rather than an object, and a wallet without address-balance support cannot show or move those funds ([Sui docs](https://docs.sui.io/onchain-finance/asset-custody/address-balances/migrate-address-balances)). Check the recipient's wallet before telling a Philippine agent that "it just arrives". An address-balance deposit also carries no memo and no per-payment object, so matching payments to invoices needs Splash's own indexer reading the protocol's balance events. Solana is stronger on this point, because its payment standard gives every transfer an indexable reference key and a memo ([Solana Pay spec](https://docs.solanapay.com/spec)).

**Move's linear types give the non-custodial claim something to stand on.** Sui's `Coin` and `Balance` values cannot be silently dropped, and `balance::destroy_zero` aborts on any leftover amount, so an auditor can check the zero-balance rule in the bytecode itself ([Sui code](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/MystenLabs/sui/main/crates/sui-framework/packages/sui-framework/sources/balance.move)). Payment Kit adds on-chain protection against paying the same invoice twice, plus receipts, at no kit fee, as long as its `registry_managed_funds` setting stays false so funds pass straight to the receiver rather than pooling in a registry ([Sui docs: Payment Kit](https://docs.sui.io/standards/payment-kit)). Because it writes objects, Payment Kit is never gasless. Use it only for invoice-bound payments that need the duplicate check, and price that cost in.

**Identity is where Sui beats both rivals outright.** zkLogin turns a Google or Apple login into a Sui address whose proof validators check on-chain, and a leaked salt "does not enable fund theft" ([Sui docs: zkLogin](https://docs.sui.io/concepts/cryptography/zklogin)). This research found no equivalent on Solana or Arbitrum. Their walletless logins rest on vendors such as Privy, whose keys are split into shares and reassembled inside AWS Nitro enclaves ([Privy](https://docs.privy.io/security/wallet-infrastructure/architecture)), which is trust in a company's infrastructure rather than in a proof. Two cautions apply. A February 2026 IACR paper found weaknesses in how zkLogin is deployed, none of them cryptographic: how login claims are parsed, impersonation across apps where the issuing context is not enforced, and centralised proving. Whether they have been patched is unknown ([IACR ePrint 2026/227](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/227)). The larger problem is Splash's own design. In the planned multisig the admin's zkLogin key and the backup passkey carry weight 2 each, while the recovery contact and **Splash's cold key carry weight 1 each against a threshold of 2**, so Splash plus the recovery contact can move a customer's money without the admin (Splash internal: `claude-code-prompt-v4-basecamp-mainnet.md`). Sui's native multisig cannot limit a key to recovery, so the non-custodial claim is not clean until that key is re-weighted or removed (K11).

## Money has no clean exit from Sui, and the main one closes on 1 December

**Circle's timetable is the biggest single risk in the Sui build.** On 3 October 2026 Circle lists **Sui (domain 8) and Noble as the only chains supported only by CCTP V1** ([Circle](https://developers.circle.com/cctp/cctp-supported-blockchains)); Aptos, the third holdout, moved to V2 in August ([Crypto Briefing](https://cryptobriefing.com/aptos-circle-cctp-v2-usdc-transfers/)). Circle cuts V1 burn limits from **31 October** and pauses the V1 contracts on **1 December**, after which "any integration still pointing at them will stop transferring USDC crosschain" ([Circle blog](https://www.circle.com/blog/migrate-to-cctp-v2-ahead-of-cctp-v1-legacy-deprecation)). Its migration guide says "Sui will be supported by V2 before the deprecation begins" ([Circle](https://developers.circle.com/cctp/migration-from-v1-to-v2.md)). Take that seriously, but do not bank on it. Circle promised V2 on Sui by the end of H1 2026 and missed ([Circle blog](https://www.circle.com/blog/cctp-version-updates)), and it is now ending USDC support on Noble, the other V1-only chain, where Coinbase halted USDC deposits and withdrawals on 17 August ([Crypto Briefing](https://cryptobriefing.com/circle-discontinues-usdc-noble-cctp-v1/)). No source suggests Sui will go the way of Noble. The real risk is narrower and still serious: even if Circle ships V2 on Sui before 31 October, Mayan, LI.FI and wallets must still move their Sui integrations onto the new contracts, so a gap of days to weeks between Circle's launch and usable routes is plausible. Burn limits start falling **28 days** after this report.

**The list of routes that survive a late V2 is short.** Mayan's standard MCTP route runs on CCTP, and its Fast MCTP route, built on V2, excludes Sui ([Mayan docs](https://docs.mayan.finance/architecture/mctp)). Mayan Swift uses solvers instead of CCTP; it charges no protocol fee, settles in seconds and handles up to about US$1M per order ([Mayan docs](https://docs.mayan.finance/llms-full.txt)). One conflict matters for exits: Mayan's docs list Sui as both a Swift source and destination, but LI.FI's route list shows Swift only *into* Sui ([LI.FI tools](https://li.quest/v1/tools?chains=9270000000000000)), so do not assume Swift can carry money out of Sui until a live test settles it. On the same LI.FI list, Allbridge's pools connect Sui to Solana, Polygon, Base, Ethereum, Tron and others but **not to Arbitrum**, and their depth is unverified. Sui's native bridge carries ETH, WBTC, LBTC and USDT from Ethereum and no USDC ([Sui docs](https://docs.sui.io/learn/sui-bridging)). Wormhole's token bridge delivers a wrapped USDC, a different asset from native USDC ([Circle](https://circle.com/blog/sui-migration-guide)), and only native USDC is on the gasless allowlist. Wormhole NTT can deploy on Sui ([Wormhole docs](https://docs.wormhole.com/products/token-transfers/native-token-transfers/guides/deploy-to-sui/)), but NTT serves issuers who control their own token, and only Circle mints USDC. deBridge and Squid do not list Sui ([deBridge](https://docs.debridge.com/dln-details/overview/fees-supported-chains.md); [Squid](https://docs.squidrouter.com/chains-and-tokens/coral-intent-swaps.md)), and Across does not appear among LI.FI's Sui routes. Exchange withdrawals of native USDC on Sui, from Coinbase and Crypto.com among others ([usdc.com](https://www.usdc.com/learn/how-to-get-usdc-on-sui)), survive, but Splash cannot program them as an exit.

**Real traffic out of Sui already looks thin.** In a 25-swap sample from Mayan's explorer, not one started on Sui ([Mayan explorer](https://explorer-api.mayan.finance/v3/swaps?sourceChain=21&limit=25)). Splash's own tests worked: Sui→Polygon turned 500 USDC into 499.82 in about 60 seconds and Solana→Sui turned 1,500 into 1,499.30 in about 60 seconds, but Arbitrum→Sui took about 20 minutes (Splash internal: `claude-code-prompt-v4-basecamp-mainnet.md`). That matches the 13–19 minute attestation wait V1 imposes when Arbitrum is the source ([Circle](https://developers.circle.com/cctp/v1/required-block-confirmations.md)). Solana and Arbitrum both run CCTP V2 with Fast Transfer ([Circle](https://developers.circle.com/cctp/cctp-supported-blockchains)), so for moving USDC between chains they are the dependable legs and Sui is the open one.

**The rest of the money picture is thin as well.** Sui's DeFi value was **US$545.7M** on 3 October, about 79% below its roughly US$2.6B peak ([DefiLlama](https://api.llama.fi/v2/chains); [Crypto Briefing](https://cryptobriefing.com/phantom-drops-sui-support-september-migration/)), and its US$478.5M stablecoin base includes US$310.5M of native USDC ([DefiLlama](https://stablecoins.llama.fi/stablecoins?includePrices=false)). USDsui has a US$76.9M market cap, about US$5.0M of daily volume and an all-time low of **US$0.9901** on 13 June 2026 ([CoinMarketCap](https://coinmarketcap.com/currencies/usdsui/)), and Coinbase does not trade it ([Coinbase](https://www.coinbase.com/en-ar/price/usdsui)). Keep it out of treasury and funding flows. The pool of payers who can pay on Sui is shrinking too. Phantom announced it would end Sui support on 24 September ([Bankless](https://www.bankless.com/read/news/phantom-to-drop-sui-in-second-chain-exit)), and MetaMask reaches Sui only through a third-party Snap with about 62K installs ([MetaMask Snaps](https://snaps.metamask.io/snap/npm/kunalabs-io/sui-metamask-snap/)).

**Reliability is the sharpest problem for B2B payments.** Sui halted three times on 28–29 May 2026, for more than 15 hours combined, and the first halt came from a bug in the Address Balances rollout that gasless transfers depend on ([Radom](https://www.radom.com/insights/recent-sui-mainnet-disruptions-linked-to-upgrade-flaw-developers-report); [CertiK](https://www.certik.com/blog/advancing-sui-the-evolution-of-suis-payment-pipeline)). CoinDesk called the 28 May stall Sui's second outage of 2026 ([CoinDesk](https://www.coindesk.com/tech/2026/05/28/sui-blockchain-suffers-another-network-outage-as-transactions-grind-to-a-halt)). No halt after May was found, but design for the next one. Show a "network stalled" state, never treat a submitted payment as settled before finality, make quotes survive a halt, and keep a way to route an urgent payout through the Solana or Arbitrum rails.

**Sui's 2026 payments partnerships point away from Southeast Asia.** They are Paga in Nigeria on USDsui, xMoney (an EU e-money institution whose round the Sui Foundation led) and Fireblocks as infrastructure ([Sui blog](https://www.sui.io/blog/shaping-the-way-money-moves-across-continents-with-sui-and-paga); [GlobeNewswire](https://www.globenewswire.com/fr/news-release/2025/09/30/3158480/0/en/xMoney-secures-21-5m-strategic-funding-led-by-Sui-Foundation.html)). The only payments-specific programme found was a four-week fellowship paying **US$25,000 per team** ([Sui blog](https://www.sui.io/blog/btcfi-payments-fellowship-on-sui)). Meanwhile Cebuana Lhuillier, with more than 3,500 Philippine branches, is building its stablecoin remittance rail on **Solana** ([Fintech News PH](https://fintechnews.ph/72356/blockchain/fireblocks-cebuana-lhuillier-stablecoin-philippines/)). A Philippines-first plan on Sui depends on Splash's own partner work, not on Sui's.

### Kaia's portal shows the right shape: own the screen, rent the rails

**Kaia's portal is worth copying for its structure, not its vendors.** Its terms say swap runs through "1inch and Swapscanner", bridging through "Stargate and Wormhole" and fiat buying through an unnamed third party; users connect their own wallet and sign every transaction, and the only eligibility control is a sanctions clause ([Kaia Portal ToS](https://portal-docs.kaia.io/terms/terms-of-service.md)). The "Powered by SwapScanner" label itself could not be seen, because the page renders only in a live browser. The terms date from August 2024, and 1inch's current chain list no longer includes Kaia ([1inch](https://business.1inch.com/portal/documentation/apis/swap/classic-swap/introduction)), so the named vendors may already be out of date. SwapScanner is a DEX aggregator for Kaia alone, routing across 17 DEXes and charging 30% of the saving it measures, capped at 0.875% of the trade, or a flat 0.3% where no comparison exists ([SwapScanner docs](https://docs.swapscanner.io/service/swap.md)); it holds about US$3.02M of value ([DefiLlama](https://defillama.com/protocol/swapscanner)) and supports none of Splash's chains, so it is irrelevant as a vendor. Kaia's own bridging guide tells users to pick the bridge that matches the DEX pool they want to use ([Kaia Portal docs](https://portal-docs.kaia.io/english/bridging-from-another-chain-to-kaia)), which is exactly the tangle of competing wrapped tokens Splash must avoid.

The deeper lesson is that the portal is Kaia's least distinctive layer. Kaia's distribution comes from LINE, where Tether's USDT on Kaia reaches about 196M monthly users ([Cointelegraph](https://cointelegraph.com/news/tether-usdt-kaia-line-stablecoin-launch)). Watch that stack as a competitor. Kaia and LINE NEXT plan PHP- and MYR-pegged stablecoins, with on- and off-ramps, inside a messenger super-app ([Decrypt](https://decrypt.co/340605/kaia-line-asia-universally-compliant-stablecoin-super-app?amp=1)), which runs against Splash's USD-first thesis in the same corridor. For now those are plans, not products.

**On Sui the same shape becomes a Fund screen that Splash owns, with every execution leg a third party named in Splash's terms.** Same-chain swaps go through the Cetus Aggregator SDK (30+ venues), 7K/Bluefin7K or the Aftermath Router, all signed by the user and all letting Splash set its partner fee to zero ([Cetus](https://github.com/CetusProtocol/aggregator); [7K](https://docs.7k.ag/7k-aggregator/integration.md); [Aftermath](https://docs.aftermath.finance/for-developers/typescript-sdk/products/router.md)). 7K takes 20% of integrator fees, which costs nothing at zero. Cetus lost about US$223M to an exploit in May 2025 ([The Defiant](https://thedefiant.io/news/hacks/cetus-protocol-hit-223-million-hack-162-million-frozen-5-million-bounty-vote-on-c13985eb)), and whether its aggregator router was exposed was not verified, so cap slippage and spread routes across venues. Cross-chain transfers go through Mayan first, which charges no protocol fee on Swift, nothing on MCTP when the output is USDC, and screens addresses against Chainalysis data ([Mayan](https://docs.mayan.finance/resources/compliance.md)). LI.FI is the fallback where a real swap is needed. Its widget can hard-lock the destination and hide the field ([LI.FI](https://docs.li.fi/widget/configure-widget)), but it adds a **0.25%** fee ([LI.FI](https://docs.li.fi/faqs/fees-monetization)), US$25 on a US$10,000 transfer, and on Sui it mostly wraps Mayan and Allbridge.

Four controls matter more than which vendor Splash picks. First, lock the destination to the user's own address, because Mayan's SDK accepts any destination ([Mayan SDK](https://docs.mayan.finance/build/sdk/executing-swaps.md)) and the lock has to be Splash's. Second, accept only native USDC as a destination token and exclude Wormhole's wrapped routes. Third, screen both addresses before building a transaction, since none of the Sui DEX aggregators screens. Fourth, require exact-amount approvals on the EVM side: the LI.FI exploit of July 2024 (about US$9M) and the Socket exploit of January 2024 both drained users who had granted unlimited approvals ([The Block](https://www.theblock.co/amp/post/305666/li-fi-loses-estimated-9-million-in-exploit); [Blockworks](https://www.blockworks.com/news/socket-bridge-protocol-exploit)). Do not copy Kaia's points missions, because incentives on a B2B settlement app raise AML and inducement questions.

## Onramper's FPX listing does not reach USDC on Sui

**FPX does appear on Onramper's Malaysia page, but nothing on the page connects it to Sui.** The page lists 14 payment methods and 11 providers without ever saying which provider handles FPX, for which asset or network, or at what price, and it still contains unfilled template placeholders ([Onramper Malaysia](https://onramper.com/country-coverage/the-best-crypto-onramps-for-malaysia)). Of the 11 providers, only TransFi was found claiming FPX on its own site, offering crypto purchases with "FPX, DuitNow QR, and Malaysian online banking". That page names no network and no fee ([TransFi MYR](https://www.transfi.com/buy-crypto/myr-to-tether)), TransFi's FPX page shows a Dubai address and no Malaysian licence ([TransFi FPX](https://www.transfi.com/payment-methods/accept-fpx)), and TransFi's USDC list covers Arbitrum and Solana but not Sui ([TransFi docs](https://docs.transfi.com/docs/supported-crypto-currencies)). On Sui specifically, Sui's documentation lists Onramper for **SUI only** ([Sui docs](https://docs.sui.io/onchain-finance/asset-custody/fiat-on-ramps)), and Onramper's swap engine, a white-label of Exodus's XOSwap, does not support Sui ([Exodus XOSwap](https://docs.exodus.com/xoswap/v4/supported-networks)), so it cannot even turn bought SUI into USDC. On today's evidence, FPX into USDC on Sui through Onramper does not exist.

**The bigger problem is legal, not technical.** None of the 11 providers on Onramper's Malaysia list appears on the Securities Commission's register of digital-asset exchanges, which named HATA Digital, Luno Malaysia, MX Global, SINEGY DAX and Kinetic DAX as of 20 July 2026 ([SC](https://www.sc.com.my/regulation/guidelines/recognizedmarkets/list-of-registered-digital-asset-exchanges)). One of them, Binance, was found by the SC in 2021 to be "operating a digital asset exchange (DAX) illegally in Malaysia" and was ordered to disable its site and apps ([Malay Mail](https://www.malaymail.com/news/money/2021/07/30/securities-commission-takes-enforcement-actions-against-binance-for-illegal/1993945)), yet Binance P2P is on Onramper's Malaysia list. A Malaysian-facing Splash app that embeds Onramper would route Malaysians to unregistered foreign operators unless it restricted providers with `onlyOnramps`, since Onramper documents no `excludeOnramps` setting ([Onramper widget](https://docs.onramper.com/docs/supported-widget-parameters.md)). FPX would matter if an SC-registered exchange, or a licensed partner in the Philippines or Indonesia, took local payment methods into USDC on a chain Splash uses. That is the thing to look for, and it is not Onramper.

**Onramper's products and prices are built for a retail wallet, not a B2B settlement app.** Its named clients are wallets and exchanges such as Coinbase Wallet, Exodus, Solflare, Trust Wallet, Bitget Wallet and Jupiter ([Onramper enterprise](https://onramper.com/enterprise)). Essentials costs **US$199 a month** with six onramps. Premium costs **US$599 a month or US$5,750 a year** and is the only plan with off-ramp, P2P, swap and custom integrator fees, while White-Label is priced on request ([Onramper pricing](https://onramper.com/pricing)). The annual Essentials price of US$1,800 is about 25% off the monthly rate, not the 16% the page claims. Onramper says it "generally does not add a fee" and is paid referral fees by providers ([Onramper enterprise](https://onramper.com/enterprise)), yet its docs FAQ says it adds no fee only for clients processing more than EUR 100,000 a month ([Onramper FAQ](https://docs.onramper.com/docs/faq)), and integrator fees pay out monthly only above a US$500 threshold ([Onramper KB](https://knowledge.onramper.com/can-i-add-a-fee-on-my-users-transactions)). Enterprise promises 24/7 support and "re-usable KYC" but publishes no SLA, uptime or revenue-share figures.

The other products disappoint in the same way. Headless Ramps is marketed as free of iframes and redirects, but the documented Checkout V2 flow creates a signed, single-use session that expires after 10 minutes and redirects the user to the provider's page ([Onramper Checkout V2](https://docs.onramper.com/docs/checkout-v2.md)). The off-ramp sends the user's crypto to a provider deposit address, and the provider pays fiat "to the user"; the docs list seven off-ramp providers where the product page claims eight, and the one published network list, Onramp Money's, has no Sui ([Onramper off-ramp flows](https://docs.onramper.com/docs/offramp-process-flow)). It cannot pay a supplier. Onramper Technologies B.V. of Amsterdam is "not party to any transactions", gives no warranties and offers no support on transactions, refunds included ([Onramper terms](https://onramper.com/terms-conditions-checkout)). No licence held by Onramper itself was found, and an unsourced opinion piece says it relies on a "software provider exemption" ([FinTelegram](https://fintelegram.com/behind-the-gateway-why-ramp-network-embraces-mica-while-onramper-operates-in-the-aggregator-loophole)). Its coverage, crypto-asset and network tables all load inside iframes that could not be read, and its live API could not be reached, so none of this could be checked against live data.

**Alchemy's alternatives page could not be read either**, because its list of "59 Onramper alternatives" loads by JavaScript ([Alchemy](https://www.alchemy.com/dapps/alternatives/onramper?size=100)). Checking the main ramps one by one gives a clear answer for Sui. Transak's API allows buying USDC on Sui ([Transak API](https://api.transak.com/api/v2/currencies/crypto-currencies)), but its Philippine methods are card and Google Pay, with card purchases capped at PHP 49,932 ([Transak fiat API](https://api.transak.com/api/v2/currencies/fiat-currencies)), and its Malaysia page shows no FPX or DuitNow ([Transak MY](https://transak.com/buy/usdc/malaysia)). Banxa lets users buy USDC on Sui but not sell it, and its Southeast Asian methods are cards plus VietQR in Vietnam ([Banxa assets](https://docs.banxa.com/products/hosted-checkout/docs/reference/supported-cryptocurrencies-and-blockchains.md); [Banxa methods](https://docs.banxa.com/products/hosted-checkout/docs/reference/supported-payment-methods.md)). ## The Philippines is where money should land, not who Splash serves

**You are half right about building offshore.** Being offshore means Malaysia does not have to be the main market, but it settles where Splash is incorporated, not where it may operate, because both candidate countries regulate by where the customer is. The Philippine SEC's rules for crypto-asset service providers apply to "all entities, local or foreign" serving people in the Philippines ([BitPinas](https://bitpinas.com/regulation/sec-clarifies/)) and require an SEC-registered corporation, a staffed local office and **PHP 100m** of paid-up capital ([CryptoSlate](https://cryptoslate.com/crypto-laws/philippines-sec-casp-rules-guidelines-2025/)). The central bank's freeze on new VASP licences is now indefinite ([Fintech News PH](https://fintechnews.ph/68110/crypto/bsp-extends-moratorium-on-new-vasp-licenses-indefinitely/)), and a 12-month freeze on new payment-operator registrations is still only a draft ([Finance Magnates](https://www.financemagnates.com/fintech/payments/philippines-plans-12-month-freeze-on-new-payment-system-operator-registrations/)). In Indonesia, Law 4/2026 lets a stablecoin be a "means of transaction" but not "a direct means of payment" ([ABNR](https://www.abnrlaw.com/news/indonesias-p2sk-law-amendment-what-law-42026-changes-for-crypto-and-digital-financial-assets)), and the regulator's blocking power now reaches foreign parties serving Indonesians ([DFDL](https://www.dfdl.com/?p=67383)). A Labuan entity gives no passport into either country. It needs Labuan FSA approval for digital financial services ([Labuan FSA](https://www.labuanfsa.gov.my/areas-of-business/financial-services/digital-financial-services)) and does not authorise serving Malaysian retail customers ([Global Law Experts](https://globallawexperts.com/labuan-company-vs-sdn-bhd-malaysia-for-fintech/)). So "the Philippines or Indonesia as main" works in one sense only: as the place money lands, paid out by a licensed partner whose own customer is the recipient.

## Walrus stays, and on Sui it is native rather than borrowed

**Walrus is the one part of the stack where Sui is structurally ahead rather than merely competitive.** On Sui the storage control plane is local. Every blob is a Sui object with a certified start and a fixed end, an auditor can verify the `BlobCertified` event as "a portable proof of availability", and anyone can re-encode a file to confirm its blob ID ([Walrus docs](https://docs.wal.app/docs/walrus-client/verifying-availability.md)). Splash's own Move modules can emit authenticated events into a per-package stream tied to checkpoints that validators sign, which a light client can verify ([Sui docs](https://docs.sui.io/develop/accessing-data/authenticated-events)). Seal's access rules are Sui Move functions that the key servers evaluate, and a requester proves who they are with a session key signed by a Sui address ([Sui docs: Seal design](https://docs.sui.io/sui-stack/seal/design)). That means a counterparty who signs in to Splash with zkLogin is already a valid Seal identity. On Solana or Arbitrum the same evidence system has to borrow from Sui: a Splash-funded Sui wallet must pay every write in WAL and SUI ([Walrus HTTP API](https://docs.wal.app/usage/web-api.html)), and every non-Sui party must be mapped to a Sui identity, which is Mysten's own pattern for EVM and Solana users ([Walrus Memory](https://docs.wal.app/walrus-memory/guides/agent-runtimes.md)).

**Splash's current code does not yet earn the claim.** `storeEncryptedInvoice` hard-codes `epochs: 5` with no renewal, which on Mainnet's two-week epochs means about **10 weeks** of storage. Seal decryption builds its session key from the operator's keypair, so the on-chain policy can only tell "operator" from "not operator", and the real access control sits in a backend allowlist. The production anchor records no intent ID, sender, recipient or amount, and a single admin key can create arbitrary anchors (Splash internal: `v10-product-engineering-memo.md`, where the anchor defects are findings M2, M5 and M6). Walrus blobs are also deletable by default, and deleting one does not remove copies already made ([Walrus HTTP API](https://docs.wal.app/usage/web-api.html); [Walrus docs](https://docs.wal.app/docs/walrus-client/managing-blobs.md)).

**The fixes are specific.** Walrus has no public publisher on Mainnet and no plans for one ([Walrus HTTP API](https://docs.wal.app/usage/web-api.html)), so Splash should run its own authenticated publisher, which accepts single-use tokens carrying size and storage-period limits and returns the blob object to a Splash address ([Walrus auth publisher](https://docs.wal.app/operator-guide/auth-publisher.html)), with Mysten's free upload relay as a fallback. Never write from server code without a relay, because a direct SDK write takes about 2,200 requests ([Mysten SDK](https://sdk.mystenlabs.com/walrus.md)). Write evidence with `permanent=true`, buy the maximum 53 epochs (about two years) and run a renewal job well before each blob expires ([Walrus network reference](https://docs.wal.app/docs/network-reference)). Seven-year retention needs at least three renewals, and wrapping blobs as shared objects lets a successor or an auditor pay for renewal if Splash disappears ([Walrus docs](https://docs.wal.app/docs/walrus-client/managing-blobs.md)). Batch documents into quilts of up to 666 files, grouped by customer and retention class, because all files in a quilt share one lifetime ([Walrus quilts](https://docs.wal.app/docs/system-overview/quilt)). Encrypt each file with its own key, wrap that key with Seal under an allowlist of Sui addresses, keep the wrapped key in Postgres, and replace operator decryption with each requester's own session key ([Sui docs: using Seal](https://docs.sui.io/sui-stack/seal/using-seal)).

For key servers, use the 5-of-8 MPC committee that its operators say went live on Mainnet on 18 June 2026 ([Ruby Nodes](https://rubynodes.io/news/article/2/seal-mpc-mainnet)), or independent servers from different operators, under signed agreements either way. Sui's own documentation conflicts on this: the Seal overview still says committee mode is Testnet-only, while the pricing page lists a 5-of-8 Mainnet committee ([Sui docs: Seal](https://docs.sui.io/sui-stack/seal); [Sui docs: Seal pricing](https://docs.sui.io/sui-stack/seal/pricing)). Each provider sets its own Seal price and none is published, and Enoki's Seal bundle is free only "for a limited time" ([Enoki pricing](https://enoki.mystenlabs.com/pricing)), so Seal remains an open cost line. Finally, anchor one Merkle root per batch through `emit_authenticated`, alongside the `RetentionCommitment` object. One evidence object per payment costs about US$0.003, against roughly **US$0.0000011 per payment** for a 100-payment batch, so the batch design is cheaper by more than three orders of magnitude (calculated from the [Sui protocol config](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/MystenLabs/sui/main/crates/sui-protocol-config/src/lib.rs) at a 100 MIST gas price taken from LI.FI and not checked against Sui itself).

**Cost is no reason to doubt Walrus.** Storage costs **US$0.023 per GB per month of encoded size**, paid in WAL ([Walrus blog](https://blog.walrus.xyz/announcing-predictable-pricing-in-usd-on-walrus/)), and encoded size is 4.5 times the file plus a fixed 64MB per blob ([Walrus docs](https://docs.wal.app/dev-guide/costs.html)). That fixed overhead is why quilts matter, but correct one earlier claim: the "about 409×" saving applies only to 10 KiB files. It falls to 190× for 50 KiB files and 13× for 1 MiB files ([Walrus quilts](https://docs.wal.app/docs/system-overview/quilt)), so typical PDF evidence saves roughly 13–100×. At an illustrative 1,000 settlements a month with 300 KB of evidence each, daily quilts cost about **US$6.30 per month of evidence over seven years**, against about US$126 if each file is stored as its own blob (my calculation). Arweave works out at about US$54 per GiB paid once, against about US$9.50 per GiB for seven years on Walrus ([Arweave](https://arweave.net/price/1073741824); [Coinbase](https://www.coinbase.com/price/arweave)), and Arweave cannot delete anything, which is a liability under erasure duties.

**What goes onto Walrus matters more than what it costs.** Walrus data is public by default and blob IDs are not secrets ([Walrus docs](https://docs.wal.app/docs/data-security.md)), Seal cannot take back a key once it has been fetched ([Sui docs](https://docs.sui.io/sui-stack/seal/security-best-practices)), and Seal's own docs say not to use it for regulated personal data ([Sui docs: Seal](https://docs.sui.io/sui-stack/seal)). The law in all three countries pulls toward deletion. Malaysia's PDPA requires personal data to be destroyed once it is no longer needed ([Chambers](https://practiceguides.chambers.com/practice-guides/data-protection-privacy-2026/malaysia)), Indonesia's PDP law gives a right to deletion ([Chambers](https://practiceguides.chambers.com/practice-guides/data-protection-privacy-2026/indonesia)), and the Philippine Data Privacy Act lets people demand destruction of their data, with exceptions for legal obligations ([NDV Law](https://ndvlaw.com/can-philippine-consumers-demand-permanent-data-deletion/)). Keep KYB files, IDs, director details and bank numbers off Walrus even when encrypted, anchor salted commitments rather than plain hashes, and treat destroying the wrapped key as the erasure mechanism, bearing in mind that no regulator in the three countries has endorsed that approach yet.

**Publish the same period root on all three chains.** Use `emit_authenticated` on Sui, a Solana Attestation Service attestation or a memo on Solana ([Solana docs](https://solana.com/docs/tools/attestations.md)), and an EAS attestation on Arbitrum One ([EAS contracts](https://github.com/ethereum-attestation-service/eas-contracts)). If Sui halts, the other two anchors still prove the evidence existed, although new writes, renewals and Seal decryption would stall until Sui restarts. Blockticity already stores certificates of origin and trade records on Walrus, with encrypted metadata and an open-source verifier that works across chains ([Walrus blog](https://blog.walrus.xyz/blockticity-brings-7b-in-authenticated-trade-records-to-walrus-verifiable-data-platform/)); read its verifier before building Splash's.

## The better plan: Sui keeps the proof, partner chains carry the payouts

This is a proposal, and it stays unconfirmed until you trigger v15. **Stop asking Sui to be the network money leaves on, and make it the ledger of record.** Accounts, limits and approvals live on Sui, using zkLogin plus passkey under native multisig with K11 fixed, and Move transactions that enforce the zero-balance rule. In-network payments stay on Sui and stay free: a Philippine agent who receives USDC on Sui and owes dollars onward to carriers can pay onward at no cost, which is where Sui's gasless transfers earn their place. Payouts go to the licensed partner on the chain it accepts. The Sui multisig approves, and a Mayan order delivers USDC straight to the partner's one-payment deposit address on Solana, Arbitrum or Polygon, so Splash never sits in the money path. Payers who already hold USDC on Solana or Arbitrum fund that address directly, and the Sui record binds the other chain's transaction hash into the evidence; be honest that on this path the Sui approval is a record, not an on-chain control. Evidence stays in one place, as Walrus quilts encrypted under Seal with one Merkle root anchored on all three chains. Funding follows Kaia's shape: a deposit QR for USDC already on Sui, Mayan for bridging, a Sui aggregator for swaps, and card or exchange routes for small top-ups, with no Onramper subscription until a provider is verified.

**Pricing keeps the line "Transfers are free. We charge for proof." and builds in Sui's mechanics.**

If counsel says a percentage of value looks like money-transmission pricing, switch the payout fee to flat bands. **Plans have to carry the revenue.** With transfers free and exits cheap, covering costs from fees alone at 0.10% needs **US$57.3M a month** of payout volume, while one Pro-size forwarder sending US$400k a month brings in about US$780 a month, so about 74 of them would cover the US$57k monthly burn (Splash internal: `vc-defense-book-2026-10-01.md`). Splash's own Sui running costs are small and known except for one. Enoki Professional costs US$120 a month including 100K sponsored transactions ([Enoki pricing](https://enoki.mystenlabs.com/pricing)), Walrus costs a few dollars a year, and address screening should be bought per check rather than as a Chainalysis contract at the roughly US$175k median ([Vendr](https://www.vendr.com/marketplace/chainalysis)). Seal has no published price yet.

**Change the claims before Basecamp.** Say "stablecoin transfers on Sui can be gas-free", never "Splash payments are gasless". Say "payouts settle through licensed partners on the chains they accept", never that Philippine partners have "no Sui". Do not say Onramper or FPX funds Sui wallets. ## Conclusion

The useful question was never whether Sui is good enough. It is where a dollar has to be when it meets a licensed partner, and on every corridor in this research the answer is a chain other than Sui, so every bank payout from money held on Sui pays for a bridge and lives on Circle's timetable. That turns the usual "why Sui" pitch around. Sui's case rests on the dollars that never need to leave: the Philippine agent paying carriers onward, the forwarder approving thirty payments in one go, the auditor checking a Walrus file against an authenticated event. The bigger that in-network loop grows, the less the exit matters, so the share of volume that stays on Sui, not a feature comparison between chains, is the number that will tell you whether Sui deserves to remain the main build.

Your four asks lead to one discipline. Kaia shows that the funding screen is a commodity to rent, Onramper shows that a long list of payment methods is not a working route, the hub research shows that "main market" means where money lands rather than who signs up, and Walrus shows that evidence is the one layer worth owning outright. Own the record, the approval and the proof, and rent the rails from whoever is licensed and already on the right chain. If Sui still has no tested exit on 31 October, the plan still works, because payouts can be funded on Solana or Arbitrum while Sui keeps the record, but from that day the pitch has to stop calling Sui the settlement rail.
