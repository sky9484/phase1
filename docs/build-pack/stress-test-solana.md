# Pay out on Solana, prove on Sui

Of Splash's three chains, Solana is the strongest at the one job a second rail exists to do, getting native USDC to licensed payout partners, and the weakest on identity, which makes it a good **second delivery rail under the Sui and Walrus record layer** as long as the Colosseum entry is rebuilt around proof of demand rather than technology. On the money side, Solana runs Circle's CCTP V2 while Sui is still on V1 only, four weeks before Circle starts throttling V1 on 31 October 2026 and eight before it pauses V1 on 1 December ([Circle](https://www.circle.com/blog/migrate-to-cctp-v2-ahead-of-cctp-v1-legacy-deprecation)); Solana holds **US$16.69B of stablecoins against Sui's US$0.48B** ([DefiLlama](https://stablecoins.llama.fi/stablecoinchains)); and PDAX, Yellow Card, TransFi, Tazapay and Fonbnk all accept USDC on Solana, while no provider was verified to pay out USDC on Sui to a Philippine, Indonesian or Nigerian bank. The gaps are just as real: GCash's crypto wallet does not take USDC on Solana, no Indonesian licensed partner was confirmed on Solana, Solana has no equivalent of zkLogin, and Seal can only grant decryption to Sui addresses. Colosseum's Crypto World's Fair closes on **12 October, nine days from now**, judges only work done since 14 September, and scores four of its seven criteria on the founder, the market, viability and traction; its US$250k accelerator invests only in full-time founders whose product has some form of Solana integration. Splash's anchor-LOI count is still zero, and Squads Altitude already runs the "non-custodial account plus licensed partners" model on Solana at **US$1.7B annualised**. Your four instincts split: Kaia's portal is the right template, but SwapScanner is a Kaia-only swap aggregator that bridges nothing; Onramper does list FPX, but no source shows FPX buying USDC on Solana and none of its Malaysian providers is registered with the Securities Commission (SC); "Philippines or Indonesia as main" is right only for where the money lands, and the Philippines does almost no business with Africa (**US$7.0m of exports to Nigeria in 2025**); and Walrus can stay, through a publisher Splash runs and pays for in SUI and WAL. The better plan is a Solana-native money path that never bridges: the payer funds in USDC on Solana, pays from a Squads vault to a licensed Philippine partner's deposit address with network fees sponsored through Kora, and Splash writes the evidence to Walrus and the transaction signature into the Sui record. Submit that with real forwarder LOIs by 12 October, and keep every price and partner claim marked unconfirmed until it is signed.

## Solana's money stack is the deepest of the three, its identity stack the thinnest

### Native USDC and CCTP V2 put Solana on the safe side of Circle's cliff

Circle issues native USDC on Solana at mint `EPjFWdd5AufqSSqeM2qN1xzybapC8G4wEGGkZwyTDt1v` ([Circle](https://developers.circle.com/stablecoins/usdc-contract-addresses)). Solana is **CCTP V2 domain 5**, with Standard Transfer, Fast Transfer and the Forwarding Service. Sui (domain 8) is "supported only by CCTP V1 (Legacy)" ([Circle](https://developers.circle.com/cctp/cctp-supported-blockchains)). Circle's schedule is firm: V1 burn limits fall from **31 October 2026**, and the V1 contracts pause on **1 December 2026**, after which "any integration still pointing at them will stop transferring USDC crosschain" ([Circle blog](https://www.circle.com/blog/migrate-to-cctp-v2-ahead-of-cctp-v1-legacy-deprecation)). Circle's migration guide says "Sui will be supported by V2 before the deprecation begins" ([Circle](https://developers.circle.com/cctp/migration-from-v1-to-v2)). Circle already missed its own target of the end of H1 2026 for Sui ([Circle blog](https://www.circle.com/blog/cctp-version-updates)). Mayan quotes Splash recorded on 28 September put 1,500 USDC from Solana to Sui at 1,499.30 out in about 60 seconds, against about 20 minutes from Arbitrum (Splash internal: `splash/vc-defense-book-2026-10-01.md`; `splash/claude-code-prompt-v4-basecamp-mainnet.md`). That route crosses the V1 boundary on the Sui side.

The cliff matters less on Solana than it first appears. **A payment that starts and ends on Solana never touches CCTP.** It only needs CCTP when money moves between Splash's rails. If the Solana rail funds in Solana USDC and pays a partner that accepts Solana USDC, the 1 December deadline does not affect the payment at all. Only the evidence record goes to Sui, and that is data paid for in SUI, not money. Moves between rails fall back on Mayan Swift (solver-based, zero protocol fee, up to about US$1M per order) and on Mayan's Fast MCTP over CCTP V2 where both chains support it ([Mayan](https://docs.mayan.finance/architecture/mctp.md)). The same reasoning helps Arbitrum, which is also on CCTP V2 (domain 3). It does nothing for Sui as a payout chain.

### Fees are tiny but not free, and every new supplier account costs rent

Solana charges a **base fee of 5,000 lamports per signature**, half burned and half paid to the validator, plus an optional priority fee ([Solana docs](https://solana.com/docs/core/fees)). The cost unique to Solana is rent. Every account must hold a deposit of (account size + 128) × 3,480 lamports per byte-year × 2 years ([Solana docs](https://solana.com/docs/core/accounts)). A USDC token account is 165 bytes, so by that formula the first payment to a new supplier wallet needs about **2,039,280 lamports, roughly 0.002 SOL**, which is about 400 times the base fee (my arithmetic). Colosseum claims storage costs "dropped 90%" without giving details, so the formula may be out of date ([Colosseum Codex](https://blog.colosseum.com/builder-station-sbpfv3-solana-resilience/)). The SOL price was not retrieved, so no US$ figure is given here. Compare the other two chains. On Sui, a bare stablecoin transfer has cost **US$0.00** since 20 May 2026, although any Splash call or evidence object makes the transaction a paid one ([Sui blog](https://www.sui.io/blog/sui-launches-gasless-stablecoin-transfers)). On Arbitrum, an ERC-20 transfer cost about **US$0.003** on 2 October 2026 ([Arbiscan](https://arbiscan.io/gastracker)). So on Solana, "transfers are free" can only mean "Splash pays the fee". The protocol does not make them free.

**Kora** is how Splash would pay those fees. It is the Solana Foundation's signing and fee-payer service. Users can "pay fees in any token—USDC, BONK, or your app's native token". It supports Token-2022 extension filtering, can sign with Turnkey, Privy or Openfort keys, and has allowlists and "rate limiting and spend protection". Runtime Verification completed its audit on 19 November 2025 ([GitHub: Kora](https://github.com/solana-foundation/kora)). The older relayer, Octane, was archived on 20 April 2026 ([GitHub: Octane](https://github.com/anza-xyz/octane)). Kora plays the role Enoki plays on Sui. Splash should pin it to an audited tagged release and sponsor fees only after KYB, with daily caps for each organisation.

### USDC cannot carry Token-2022 rules, so memo discipline is Splash's job

Solana's Token-2022 programme offers extensions Splash would want: MemoTransfer ("Require inbound transfers to have memo"), Transfer Hook, Confidential Transfer, Permanent Delegate and Pausable ([Solana docs](https://solana.com/docs/tokens/extensions)). None of them helps with USDC. **Native USDC on Solana is widely understood to be a classic SPL Token mint, not a Token-2022 mint.** The research did not confirm this from a primary source, so check it with one RPC call that reads the owner programme of the mint account. If it holds, Splash cannot force a memo, add a transfer hook or use confidential transfers at the token level. Confidential transfers were switched off in June 2025, and the ZK ElGamal proof programme behind them was disabled on 19 June, after a proof-forgery flaw with "no known exploits" ([Solana Foundation](https://solana.com/news/post-mortem-june-25-2025)). Two secondary sources report it re-enabled in 2026, but they disagree on the version ([Solana Compass](https://solanacompass.com/news/confidential-transfers-return-to-solana-mainnet-after-year-long-security-pause)). Either way, the re-enablement does not apply to USDC.

The practical answer is Solana Pay's conventions, applied in Splash's own transaction builder. A transfer request carries `recipient`, `amount`, `spl-token`, `reference`, `label`, `message` and `memo`. A `reference` is a 32-byte key that can be found with `getSignaturesForAddress`, and the memo sits immediately before the token transfer ([Solana Pay](https://docs.solanapay.com/spec)). That gives D9's "pay link on an invoice" a native Solana form. The memo programme accepts up to 566 bytes ([Solana Program Library](https://www.solana-program.com/docs/memo)). **Memos are public and permanent**, so they should carry only an opaque Splash record ID or salted hash, never a name, invoice number or amount narrative.

The **Solana Attestation Service (SAS)** has been live since 23 May 2025. Members of the Solana Identity Group include Civic, Solid and the Solana Foundation, and Sumsub is among the teams building on it. Its stated use is reusable "KYC passports" ([Solana Foundation](https://solana.com/news/solana-attestation-service)), with "no personal info onchain" ([Blockworks](https://www.blockworks.com/news/solana-attestation-service-sas-permissions)). Its credentials, schemas and expiring attestations ([Solana docs](https://solana.com/docs/tools/attestations/overview)) make it the natural home for a "KYB passed" flag bound to a business wallet. Arbitrum's equivalent is EAS. This research found no Sui counterpart.

### Squads, passkeys and SAS replace Sui's authority model; nothing replaces zkLogin

Authority maps across cleanly. Solana's secp256r1 precompile (SIMD-0075) is "Implemented" ([SIMD-0075](https://github.com/solana-foundation/solana-improvement-documents/blob/main/proposals/0075-precompile-for-secp256r1-sigverify.md)) and was enabled in June 2025 ([Helius](https://www.helius.dev/blog/solana-passkeys)), so passkeys can sign on-chain. **LazorKit** is a passkey smart wallet on mainnet. Accretion audited its V2 programme with all 17 issues resolved, it uses Kora for gasless transactions, and it recovers through iCloud Keychain or Google Password Manager ([Solana Compass](https://solanacompass.com/projects/lazorkit)). **Squads V4** is the multisig standard. It offers time locks, spending limits, roles and sub-accounts, and has audits from OtterSec, Neodyme and Trail of Bits plus Certora formal verification ([GitHub: Squads V4](https://github.com/Squads-Protocol/v4)). The newer Squads Smart Account programme deploys wallets for "as little as 0,0000025 SOL" and defers rent until the account first executes ([GitHub: Squads Smart Account](https://github.com/Squads-Protocol/smart-account-program)). Squads' roles and time locks may also give Splash a tool that Sui's native multisig lacks: a recovery key that cannot approve payments. That could close the K11 hole in the current multisig plan, where Splash's cold key plus the recovery contact reach the signing threshold without the business admin (Splash internal: `splash/claude-code-prompt-v4-basecamp-mainnet.md`). This is my inference, and the exact configuration needs a technical spike.

Identity has no equivalent. **No Solana equivalent of zkLogin was found.** On Sui, the address comes from the OAuth token, and validators check that link with a zero-knowledge proof. On Solana, OAuth or email login comes from embedded-wallet vendors. Privy splits keys using Shamir's secret sharing, keeping one share inside AWS Nitro enclaves ([Privy](https://docs.privy.io/security/wallet-infrastructure/architecture)), and Stripe agreed to acquire it in June 2025 ([Decrypt](https://decrypt.co/324674/payments-giant-stripe-acquire-crypto-firm-privy)). Turnkey runs keys in secure enclaves under QuorumOS ([Turnkey](https://docs.turnkey.com/security/our-approach)). That is a vendor-trust model, not a cryptographic one. **"Non-custodial" holds only if the user's own authentication factor is required to sign.** A key quorum that Splash's server can complete alone would read as Splash controlling client keys. Arbitrum has exactly the same gap.

### Solana has not halted since February 2024, and Alpenglow lands in the deadline month

Solana had seven full outages between December 2020 and February 2024. The last one, on **6 February 2024**, lasted about five hours and was caused by an "infinite recompile loop in JIT cache" ([Helius](https://www.helius.dev/blog/solana-outages-complete-history); [The Block](https://www.theblock.co/post/276237/solana-network-restarts-after-outage-that-lasted-five-hours)). That gives about 32 months without a halt. The worst incident since was on 12 August 2026, when a TeraSwitch routing failure took about 29% of stake offline. "Block production never stopped", recovery took a little over 30 minutes, and Colosseum, the only source found, says no users saw an impact ([Colosseum Codex](https://blog.colosseum.com/builder-station-sbpfv3-solana-resilience/)). Compare the others. Sui stalled three times on 28–29 May 2026, for **more than 15 hours** in total, because of a bug in the Address Balances feature that its gasless transfers depend on ([Radom](https://www.radom.com/insights/recent-sui-mainnet-disruptions-linked-to-upgrade-flaw-developers-report)). CoinDesk called the first of those stalls Sui's second outage of the year ([CoinDesk](https://www.coindesk.com/tech/2026/05/28/sui-blockchain-suffers-another-network-outage-as-transactions-grind-to-a-halt)). Arbitrum's status history shows no sequencer outage from May to October 2026, but it does show a 9-hour-19-minute delay in posting assertions on 13 May ([Arbitrum status](https://status.arbitrum.io/history)). Arbitrum also relies on a single sequencer, with forced inclusion through Ethereum taking up to a day ([L2BEAT](https://l2beat.com/scaling/projects/arbitrum)). On this evidence, **Solana's recent record is at least as good as Arbitrum's and far better than Sui's this year**, which reverses its old reputation. Arbitrum's history before May 2026 was not verified.

The open risk is timing. **Alpenglow**, Solana's new consensus, has run on a community cluster since May 2026, and "Anza will announce the mainnet-beta migration after an observation period" ([Colosseum Codex](https://blog.colosseum.com/cohort-v-demo-day-solana-microscope-alpenglow-migration/)). Full activation was expected in October ([Crypto Briefing](https://cryptobriefing.com/jump-firedancer-ends-frankendancer-support/)), and it was not confirmed live on 3 October. Full Firedancer has run since December 2025 and holds about 11–14% of stake (same source). Do not quote 150 ms finality or "sub-second settlement" in the pitch. Build retry handling for expired blockhashes, and re-test it after Alpenglow activates. Ecosystem services can also disappear: the Switchboard oracle ended support on 25 September 2026 ([Colosseum Codex](https://blog.colosseum.com/cohort-v-demo-day-solana-microscope-alpenglow-migration/)).

## Kaia's portal and Onramper both sell a menu, not a route

### Kaia's lesson is the shell, not SwapScanner

The Kaia Portal is run by the Kaia DLT Foundation under Abu Dhabi law. Every execution leg is a named third party. Its terms define Swap as running "through Third Party Services, 1inch and Swapscanner", Bridge as running through "Stargate and Wormhole", and Buy as a fiat purchase through an unnamed provider. Users connect their own wallets and sign every transaction. The only compliance gate is a sanctions clause, and no KYC, transaction screening or fees are described ([Kaia Portal ToS](https://portal-docs.kaia.io/terms/terms-of-service.md)). Usage is driven by a points and "Missions" programme ([Kaia Portal docs](https://portal-docs.kaia.io/)). The bridging guide tells users to pick the bridge that matches the liquidity pool they want ([Kaia Portal docs](https://portal-docs.kaia.io/english/bridging-from-another-chain-to-kaia)). That is wrapped-token fragmentation exposed directly in the interface.

**SwapScanner does not bridge, and it is not a vendor Splash could use.** It is a swap aggregator for Kaia only, combining 17 Kaia exchanges. It charges "30% of the amount saved" capped at 0.875%, or a flat 0.3% when there is no price comparison ([SwapScanner docs](https://docs.swapscanner.io/service/swap.md)). It held about US$3M of TVL on 3 October ([DefiLlama](https://defillama.com/protocol/swapscanner)), and since 2022 its quote API has powered swaps inside the Klip, Kaikas and AhnLab ABC wallets ([SwapScanner history](https://docs.swapscanner.io/roadmap-and-history/update-history.md)). The live page could not be rendered, so the "Powered by SwapScanner" label was not seen. The 1inch line in Kaia's terms may be stale, because 1inch's current API chain list omits Kaia ([1inch](https://business.1inch.com/portal/documentation/apis/swap/classic-swap/introduction)).

What to copy is the structure. Build your own interface, have the user sign, name every provider in the terms, and buy the execution layer instead of building it. On Solana, that layer is **Jupiter** for same-chain swaps and **Mayan** for moves between chains. Jupiter's Ultra API "is no longer actively maintained and has been superseded by Swap V2" ([Jupiter](https://developers.jup.ag/docs/ultra/fees)). Swap V2 charges **0 bps on pegged pairs**, 2 bps for SOL against a stablecoin and 10 bps for other pairs. It lets the integrator set a custom `receiver` and requires the user to sign ([Jupiter](https://developers.jup.ag/docs/swap/order-and-execute.md)). Mayan charges no protocol fee on Swift, and none on MCTP when the output is USDC. Fast MCTP over CCTP V2 costs 3 bps ([Mayan](https://docs.mayan.finance/architecture/mctp.md)). Mayan screens transactions with Chainalysis data ([Mayan](https://docs.mayan.finance/resources/compliance.md)), and Sui, Solana and Arbitrum are all active as both origin and destination ([Mayan](https://sia.mayan.finance/v10/init)). deBridge charges 4 bps plus 0.015 SOL on Solana ([deBridge](https://docs.debridge.com/dln-details/overview/fees-supported-chains.md)). LI.FI adds 0.25% but provides a widget with a locked `toAddress` ([LI.FI](https://docs.li.fi/faqs/fees-monetization); [LI.FI](https://docs.li.fi/widget/configure-widget)).

Do not copy three things. Kaia documents no transaction screening, so Splash must screen both addresses itself, especially because Mayan's `destinationWalletAddress` accepts any address ([Mayan](https://docs.mayan.finance/build/sdk/executing-swaps.md)). Kaia's interface exposes several wrapped variants of the same token, so Splash should allow native USDC as the only destination token. And Kaia's points programme has no place in B2B payments, where incentives raise AML and inducement concerns. For payers funding from EVM wallets, require exact-amount approvals. The Socket, LI.FI and SwapNet exploits all drained wallets that had granted unlimited approvals ([The Block](https://www.theblock.co/amp/post/305666/li-fi-loses-estimated-9-million-in-exploit)). Kaia also sends one competitive signal. With LINE, it plans a messenger super-app that supports PHP, MYR and IDR stablecoins ([Decrypt](https://decrypt.co/340605/kaia-line-asia-universally-compliant-stablecoin-super-app?amp=1)), which cuts against Splash's USD-first thesis. Its USDT already reaches GCash, whose GCrypto wallet lists USDT on Kaia ([GCash](https://help.gcash.com/hc/en-us/articles/9781218166041-GCrypto-coins-and-networks)).

### Onramper lists FPX; nothing shows FPX buying USDC on Solana

**Your "FPX is massive" claim is literally true and commercially unproven.** Onramper's Malaysia page lists 11 providers and 14 payment methods, FPX and DuitNow among them. Nothing on the page says which provider handles FPX, for which token or network, or at what fee, limit or KYC level ([Onramper Malaysia](https://onramper.com/country-coverage/the-best-crypto-onramps-for-malaysia)). ### "Philippines or Indonesia as main" works only where the money lands

You are half right. Building offshore does not force Malaysia to be the main market. But **a Labuan entity gives no passport into either country**, because both regulate by where the customer is. Labuan entities need Labuan FSA approval for digital financial services and must keep real premises and staff in Labuan ([Labuan FSA](https://www.labuanfsa.gov.my/areas-of-business/financial-services/digital-financial-services); [Global Law Experts](https://globallawexperts.com/labuan-company-vs-sdn-bhd-malaysia-for-fintech/)).

The Philippine door is shut to new licensees. The BSP extended its freeze on new VASP licences **indefinitely**, as reported on 2 September 2025 ([Fintech News PH](https://fintechnews.ph/68110/crypto/bsp-extends-moratorium-on-new-vasp-licenses-indefinitely/)). A 12-month freeze on new payment-operator registrations is still only a draft ([Finance Magnates](https://www.financemagnates.com/fintech/payments/philippines-plans-12-month-freeze-on-new-payment-system-operator-registrations/)). The SEC's crypto-asset service provider (CASP) rules apply to "all entities, local or foreign" serving people in the Philippines and require a Philippine corporation, a staffed local office and **₱100m of paid-up capital** ([CryptoSlate](https://cryptoslate.com/crypto-laws/philippines-sec-casp-rules-guidelines-2025/); [BitPinas](https://bitpinas.com/regulation/sec-clarifies/)).

Indonesia's Law 4/2026 says a stablecoin "cannot serve as a direct means of payment" ([ABNR](https://www.abnrlaw.com/news/indonesias-p2sk-law-amendment-what-law-42026-changes-for-crypto-and-digital-financial-assets)). It also brings foreign parties serving Indonesians within OJK's enforcement reach ([DFDL](https://www.dfdl.com/?p=67383)).

The only clean way to make either country "main" is as the **destination**. The recipient onboards with the licensed local partner, and Splash serves only the payer outside that country.

On that definition, the ranking is the same for all three chains: **the Philippines is the main destination market, Indonesia is the second destination, and Malaysia stays the origin and customer base**, subject to the open K10 counsel question.

The Philippines ranks first because it has at least seven licensed VASPs, two of them banks and several with live stablecoin products ([BitPinas](https://bitpinas.com/feature/list-licensed-virtual-currency-exchanges-philippines/)). It also has more than US$40bn of IT-BPM export revenue ([Philstar](https://www.philstar.com/business/2026/01/29/2504140/it-bpm-revenues-breach-40-billion-mark/amp/)) and the only live e-invoicing deadline among the three countries: BIR's **31 December 2026** for identified taxpayers ([Grant Thornton PH](https://www.grantthornton.com.ph/insights/articles-and-updates1/lets-talk-tax/the-ber-months-are-here-the-countdown-to-the-e-invoicing-deadline-on-31-december-2026/)).

Indonesia's pool is as large or larger, and Malaysia→Indonesia costs average 4.80% ([World Bank RPW](https://remittanceprices.worldbank.org/corridor/Malaysia/Indonesia)). It ranks second because its partner stack runs through an OJK sandbox entity, splits across two licence types, and faces Airwallex, which announced in December 2025 that it had bought a majority stake in a Category 1 payment licence holder ([Airwallex](https://www.airwallex.com/newsroom/airwallex-accelerates-asia-pacific-growth-with-an-acquisition-of-a-majority)).

Singapore, Thailand and Vietnam are not viable now. Since 30 June 2025, Singapore has made Singapore-based firms that serve only overseas customers stop unless they hold a licence that MAS "will generally not issue" ([CMS](https://cms.law/en/sgp/legal-updates/singapore-s-june-30-hard-deadline-for-crypto-firms-serving-overseas-customers)). Vietnam's pilot is in dong only and excludes fiat-backed tokens ([US-ASEAN](https://usasean.org/article/vietnam-launches-five-year-pilot-program-regulate-crypto-trading)). Thailand allows USDC only as a trading pair on licensed exchanges ([Thailand Business News](https://www.thailand-business-news.com/banking/201121-thai-sec-broadens-cryptocurrency-offerings-by-adding-stablecoins-usdc-and-usdt)).

The first corridor is **Malaysia→Philippines B2B: Malaysian freight forwarders paying Philippine destination agents**, with a licensed Philippine VASP paying out in pesos. The second is **Malaysia→Indonesia B2B supplier payments** on the DurianPay pilot design.

What this means for Solana is plain. **The Solana rail is the natural carrier for corridor one and a poor one for corridor two.** PDAX accepts Solana USDC and Yellow Card pays pesos from it, while DurianPay does not list Solana. Run the Indonesian leg on whichever chain the Indonesian partner confirms in writing.

## Walrus stays, but every Solana evidence write is a Sui transaction Splash pays for

Walrus works from Solana at the transport level and is Sui-only underneath. Any backend can write and read over HTTP or an SDK. But every write is a Sui transaction flow paid in WAL for storage and SUI for gas. **Mainnet has no public unauthenticated publisher, and "there are no plans to create one"** ([Walrus docs](https://docs.wal.app/usage/web-api.html)). No official Walrus–Solana integration, bridge or partnership was found. The only Solana-facing statements are general marketing about any chain using Walrus ([Solana Compass](https://solanacompass.com/projects/walrus); [Walrus](https://www.walrus.xyz/walrus-for-web3-builders)).

The pattern that fits Splash is a **Splash-run authenticated publisher**. Splash funds it from its own Sui "evidence treasury" wallet, and the Solana backend issues a one-time token for each upload. Walrus documents such tokens: they carry `max_size`, `max_epochs`, `exp` and a single-use `jti`, and a `send_object_to` parameter returns the blob object to a Splash-controlled Sui address ([Walrus](https://docs.wal.app/operator-guide/auth-publisher.html)). Mysten's free Mainnet upload relay is the fallback, because a direct SDK write takes about 2,200 requests ([Mysten SDK](https://sdk.mystenlabs.com/walrus.md); [Walrus](https://docs.wal.app/docs/system-overview/relay)). End users never touch SUI or WAL. This is Mysten's own reference design for non-Sui users. Walrus Memory says "an Ethereum address cannot own a memory account" and tells developers to keep the mapping between other-chain identities and Sui identities in their own database ([Walrus Memory](https://docs.wal.app/walrus-memory/guides/agent-runtimes.md)). Blockticity's trade-certificate records with a "multi-chain verifier" are the closest public precedent ([Walrus blog](https://blog.walrus.xyz/blockticity-brings-7b-in-authenticated-trade-records-to-walrus-verifiable-data-platform/)).

Seal is the harder dependency. Access is decided by a Move `seal_approve` function, and the requester proves identity with a **session key signed by a Sui address** ([Sui docs](https://docs.sui.io/sui-stack/seal/design); [Sui docs](https://docs.sui.io/sui-stack/seal/using-seal)). Splash has three options. Option A is today's code: `liveSealAdapter.decrypt` builds its session key from the operator's keypair, so the on-chain policy can only tell "operator" from "not operator", and real access control sits in a backend allowlist (Splash internal: `splash/v10-product-engineering-memo.md`). That means "trust Splash's server", which is fine for a demo and weak for the moat claim. Option B gives each Solana payer, recipient and auditor a **Sui identity through zkLogin and Enoki** (a Google sign-in), lists those addresses in the Seal allowlist, and maps them to Solana wallets in Postgres. Mysten's Walrus Console does the same for non-crypto users ([Walrus Console](https://docs.wal.app/docs/console/overview.md)). Option B is the recommendation. It also means zkLogin stays in Splash's Solana story, as the identity for evidence access rather than for money. Option C is a custom `seal_approve` that verifies a Solana ed25519 signature with Sui's `ed25519_verify` ([Sui docs](https://docs.sui.io/guides/developer/cryptography/signing)). Mysten does not document it, so treat it as R&D, not canon.

Seal has been on Mainnet since September 2025 ([BlockEden](https://blockeden.xyz/blog/2025/09/03/seal-onchain-secrets-layer/)) and has had a 5-of-8 MPC committee since 18 June 2026 ([Ruby Nodes](https://rubynodes.io/news/article/2/seal-mpc-mainnet)). Its prices are not published ([Sui docs](https://docs.sui.io/sui-stack/seal/pricing)). It "cannot take back keys a user already fetched" ([Sui docs](https://docs.sui.io/sui-stack/seal/security-best-practices)), and its own docs say not to store "regulated personal data" with it ([Sui docs](https://docs.sui.io/sui-stack/seal)).

On Solana, the evidence anchor can take four forms. Splash should use two of them: the memo or reference as a per-payment breadcrumb, and SAS for the KYB credential and for each period's Merkle root.

| Anchor on Solana | Holds | Cost | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| SPL Memo in the payment transaction | Opaque record ID or salted hash, up to 566 bytes; public and permanent | Transaction fee only | Per-payment breadcrumb |
| Solana Pay `reference` key | 32-byte lookup key | None extra | Invoice pay links and reconciliation |
| SAS attestation | Structured schema, issuer, expiry | Rent, not measured | KYB credential; period Merkle root |
| Splash programme account | Hash, blob ID, metadata (~120 bytes) | ~0.0017 SOL refundable rent (docs formula) | Only if on-chain queries are needed; adds audit surface |

The loop closes in both directions. The evidence is Seal-encrypted and stored on Walrus, and its blob ID and hash go into Splash's Sui record. The Solana payment carries only the opaque ID. The Solana transaction signature is then written back into the Sui record. Publish the same period Merkle root on all three chains: Sui authenticated events ([Sui docs](https://docs.sui.io/develop/accessing-data/authenticated-events)), SAS on Solana, and EAS on Arbitrum. If Sui halts, the Solana anchor still proves the evidence existed at that time. That redundancy matters on the Solana path. **A Sui halt stalls evidence writes and Seal decryption, but not the payment itself.** Anchors should queue and catch up.

The costs are trivial and the defaults are dangerous. Since 13 May 2026, storage costs **US$0.023 per GB per month of encoded size**, paid in WAL ([Walrus blog](https://blog.walrus.xyz/announcing-predictable-pricing-in-usd-on-walrus/)). Encoded size is 4.5 times the raw size plus 64 MB per blob ([Walrus docs](https://docs.wal.app/dev-guide/costs.html)). Batched into daily quilts, an illustrative 1,000 settlements a month with 300 KB of evidence each comes to about US$6 per monthly cohort over seven years (my arithmetic). Quilts save 409 times only for 10 KiB files and about 13 times for 1 MiB files ([Walrus docs](https://docs.wal.app/docs/system-overview/quilt)), so any "about 400 times" claim needs that caveat. Blobs are **deletable by default**, and deletion does not guarantee removal ([Walrus docs](https://docs.wal.app/docs/walrus-client/managing-blobs.md)). A blob can be bought at most 53 two-week epochs ahead, about two years ([Walrus](https://docs.wal.app/docs/network-reference)), so seven-year retention needs scheduled renewals. Splash's code hardcodes `epochs: 5` with no renewal (Splash internal: `splash/v10-product-engineering-memo.md`), which is about ten weeks on Mainnet. Write evidence with `permanent=true`, buy the maximum number of epochs, use shared blobs so a successor or auditor could fund extensions, and run a renewal job. Never put KYB documents, IDs, account numbers or individuals' personal data on Walrus, even encrypted ([Walrus docs](https://docs.wal.app/docs/data-security.md)). Keep the Seal-wrapped key in Postgres so it can be destroyed for erasure. On Walrus, Solana is no weaker than Arbitrum and both are weaker than Sui: each needs the Sui-side publisher, Sui identities and Sui uptime for evidence.

The redesign below is a proposal. It stays unconfirmed until you trigger the next canon version, and Sebastian must confirm every change to the Move contracts and the Solana programme. It rests on one structural idea. **Solana is a second delivery rail under the same record layer, so money never has to leave it.** A Malaysian forwarder's Squads vault funds in USDC on Solana, either from a Solana wallet such as Phantom (which left Sui on 24 September but remains a Solana wallet; [Crypto Briefing](https://cryptobriefing.com/phantom-drops-sui-support-september-migration/)) or from an exchange withdrawal. Approvers sign with passkeys once the Squads-passkey spike passes, and with ordinary member keys until then. Kora pays the network fee, and Splash funds the first-payment account rent. The USDC goes to a licensed Philippine partner's per-payment deposit address, with a memo holding only Splash's opaque record ID. The partner pays pesos to the agent's named account. Splash's backend writes the evidence pack to Walrus through its own publisher, anchors the hash and the Solana signature on Sui, and adds the payment to that period's Merkle root on all three chains. Nothing crosses CCTP. Sui carries the record. Splash holds no balance, and its only signature on the payment is Kora's fee-payer signature, which cannot move the payer's funds. That is a real Solana settlement flow, so it meets Colosseum's integration test. It matches the canon's "record on Sui, settle on whatever rail the counterparty already uses" (Splash internal: `splash/usp-and-repositioning.md`). And it answers the Altitude question with the two things Altitude lacks: Philippine destination payouts and evidence an auditor can check.

The Colosseum entry should be that one flow and nothing more. Use devnet USDC and a **clearly labelled mock partner deposit address**, because no partner is signed and none may be named as one. Walrus Testnet is acceptable for the demo if it is labelled, since its data can be wiped without warning (Splash internal: `splash/v10-product-engineering-memo.md`). Add three to ten forwarder conversations converted into LOIs. Add a go-to-market plan that starts with Malaysian forwarders paying Philippine agents before BIR's deadline, and explain that recipients join through the licensed partner. Leave out consumer remittance, Africa, "60–90% fewer flags", "gasless payments" and any finality figure.

Fees should follow the D10 and D11 proposals and stay the same on every chain, so pricing does not depend on which rail carries a payment. The pitch line still holds: **"Transfers are free. We charge for proof."** The honest all-in price on a peso payout is **about 0.30–0.35% on tickets of US$2,000 and above**, and only if the partner's real rate is about 0.20% or less. That sits at the floor of the B2B market (Wise's best case is 0.23%), not under it (Splash internal: `splash/vc-defense-book-2026-10-01.md`). A 0.10% all-in fee is impossible on any fiat payout.

## Conclusion

The three-chain question turns out to be a question about where the licensed exits are, and today Solana wins it. Philippine and African payout providers take USDC on Solana, Circle has already moved Solana to CCTP V2, and Solana has gone longer without a halt than Sui. Sui still wins the record: Walrus, Seal and zkLogin are native there and nowhere else. Splitting the jobs that way does more than satisfy the framing rule. It removes Sui's two biggest liabilities from the payment path, the 1 December bridge cliff and the halt risk, while keeping the evidence layer, which is Splash's most defensible difference from Squads Altitude. It also means that in this design a Sui halt delays reconciliation rather than payment.

The harder truth is that Colosseum will not reward the architecture. It will reward proof that Malaysian forwarders want this and that a licensed Philippine partner will pay out for it, and Splash has neither in writing. The better plan is therefore narrow. Submit one Solana-native flow that pays a labelled partner address from a Squads vault, with evidence on Walrus and the record on Sui. Turn forwarder calls into LOIs before 12 October. Use the next eight weeks to get one Philippine provider to agree in writing to per-payment deposit addresses on Solana. A single signed exit on the rail you are demoing is worth more to judges, investors and the canon than any number of chains.
